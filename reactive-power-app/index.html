STEP 4 Inverter fleet & compensation

No power-factor knob: each inverter simply dispatches the active power the plant needs — (grid limit + losses) ÷ count — and its reactive capability follows from that loading. Enter the number of inverters your plant has, or leave it blank to use the recommended count: the smallest fleet that covers both the active delivery and the reactive demand.

Reactive capability at the dispatch point comes from the Step 5 capability table (1.00 Vn row) when it is in use; otherwise from the manual kVAr value, or the circle √(S² − P²). A capacitor bank supplies part of the reactive demand directly, so it reduces the share the inverters must cover. More inverters ⇒ lighter loading each ⇒ more reactive headroom — the recommended count is where both requirements are first met.

STEP 6 Reactive capability vs grid voltage

Grid codes require reactive support across a voltage band, not just at nominal. With the Step 5 table in use, each voltage level reads the inverter's capability from the table at the dispatch loading. Where the table does not permit that loading at a voltage, active power is backed off to the maximum permitted point and the reactive capability there is used — the row shows the reduced achievable P (⚠) and an amber “Q met · P curtailed” status, rather than zeroing the capability. Otherwise a current-limit model applies: apparent power shrinks with voltage below 1.00 p.u. (S = V × Srated) and holds rated above.

STEP 7 Grid-code compliance diagrams

The two assessment curves of a G99 Type C reactive power study. V–Q at Registered Capacity: the requirement polygon (dashed) against the plant's capability envelope at full output across the voltage range. P–Q at nominal voltage: the requirement envelope against plant capability over the whole output range. Capability is at the point of interconnection — net of transformer consumption on the export side, aided by it on the absorption side; inverter capability is taken as symmetric and the capacitor bank counts for export only.
